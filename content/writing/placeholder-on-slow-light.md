The first time I learned that starlight is old, it felt like being let in on a secret that everyone else already knew. The light reaching your eye from a bright star tonight left it years ago, sometimes centuries ago. You are not seeing the star. You are reading its mail, postmarked long before you were born, delivered with perfect patience to a person it had no way of knowing would exist.

That fact has stuck with me for a long time, mostly because it keeps turning out to be useful. Not as astronomy, which I know only a little about, but as a way of thinking about things that take a long time to arrive.

## The delay is the message

We tend to treat delay as a defect. A slow page load, a reply that takes a week, a skill that refuses to improve on schedule: all of these feel like something went wrong in transit. But with starlight, the delay is not noise on top of the signal. The delay *is* information. Astronomers use the travel time to measure distance, and the stretching of the light along the way to measure how fast the universe is expanding.[^1]

I find that reassuring. It suggests that the gap between doing something and seeing its effect is not wasted time. It is where most of the interesting things happen.

> Everything you see in the night sky is the past, arriving on time.

Consider how this plays out with something ordinary, like learning an instrument. For the first few months, practice feels like shouting into a void. You put in the hours and the music that comes out sounds roughly the same as it did before. Then, one evening, a passage that has been impossible for weeks simply works, as though it had been on its way the whole time and only now crossed the distance.

### Three kinds of slow

Not all slowness is the same. When something is taking a long time, it helps to ask which kind it is:

1. **Travel time.** The work is done; the result just hasn't arrived. Waiting is the right move.
2. **Accumulation.** Each small effort adds a little, and nothing is visible until enough has piled up. Consistency matters more than intensity.
3. **Wrong direction.** You are moving, but away from where you want to be. Here, patience is the mistake.

The trouble is that from the inside, all three feel identical. They all feel like nothing is happening.

## Reading old light

There is a practical habit hidden in all of this. When I look back at notes I wrote a year ago, I try to read them the way you might look at a distant star: as an accurate picture of something that has since changed. The person who wrote them was not wrong, exactly. They were just further away.

This makes it easier to be generous with old work. A first draft is not an embarrassment; it is a measurement. It tells you how far you have travelled. If I could not see any distance between who I was and who I am, that would be the worrying sign.

It also makes it easier to be patient with new work. Some of what I am doing now will only make sense later, when it arrives. I have started keeping a short list of things I am deliberately not judging yet:

- habits that are only a few weeks old
- projects that are still in the "pile up enough" phase
- opinions I formed recently and have not had a chance to test
- anything I started after midnight

None of this means waiting forever. It means giving things a fair amount of time to cross the distance before deciding they never left.

## When the light runs out

There is a less comfortable side to the metaphor, and it would be dishonest to leave it out. Because starlight is old, some of the stars you can see may no longer be there. Their light is still travelling, still arriving, still perfectly convincing, long after the source has changed or gone out entirely.[^2]

People and habits work this way too. You can keep receiving the light of something that ended a while ago: a friendship that drifted, a goal you quietly stopped caring about, a version of yourself that you still introduce as current. The signal is real. The source is not.

I do not think the answer is to stop looking up. The answer is to check, now and then, whether what you are seeing is still there. Ask the friend. Revisit the goal. Read the old notes and notice the distance. Some of the light will turn out to be from stars that are burning brighter than ever. Some of it will not.

### A small checklist

When something feels stuck, I have started running through a few questions. They are not clever, but they help:

- Has enough time passed for the result to arrive? (Travel time.)
- Have I done enough of it for anything to be visible yet? (Accumulation.)
- If I kept going exactly like this for a year, would I end up somewhere I want to be? (Direction.)
- Is the thing I am waiting on still there at all?

If you want the tool version of this, it is barely a tool: a note titled `not-yet.md` with a date next to each item. When the date passes, I look again. Most of the time the answer is "keep going". Occasionally it is "this stopped existing a while ago, and you were still waving at it".

## The view from here

On a clear night, the sky is a collage of different moments. Light from a nearby star left a few years ago; light from the faint smudge of another galaxy left before there were people to see it. All of it arrives at once, layered into a single picture that never actually existed at any one time.

I like thinking of a life the same way. The things that shaped you did not all happen at once, but they all arrive together, in whatever you are doing today. Some of it is light you sent yourself a long time ago and are only now receiving. Some of it you are sending now, and will not see for years.

That is, I think, the real comfort in slow light. Not that everything arrives eventually, because some things never do. But that the waiting is not empty. It is distance being crossed, by something that is already on its way.
