Twice a day, at 11:11, my friend and I message each other: “wish!”

It began as something small. When the numbers lined up, it felt harmless to believe in something, just for a minute. We’d make a wish, sometimes a prayer for a test, other times for nothing at all, and then return to whatever we were doing. Over time, it became routine. Even now, with both of us in college and nearly a thousand miles apart, nothing’s changed. Same minute, different places. Sometimes one of us forgets. Sometimes we’re busy. But more often than not, at 11:11, one of us remembers. I didn’t think much of it at first. I never had much to wish for anyway.

## Space, then time

There’s a saying that high school friendships don’t last, that college is where you find the people who stay. I’ve heard it enough times that it feels almost factual, like something proven. But I’ve started to wonder whether friendships fail because of when they were formed, or because of what happens after the structures around them disappear. In school, friendships are held together by shared space and shared time — lunch, clubs, classes that meet at the same hour every day. When those dissolve, so does the effortlessness of staying close. I’ve heard some say that if a friendship is real, it shouldn’t require maintenance. That true friendship should survive distance on its own.

When people disperse, space is the first thing to go. Then time follows. Schedules diverge, and lives move forward at different speeds. Reunions try to give both back at once, the same room at the same moment, but only briefly, and then everyone leaves and the timelines split again.

> [!pull] Same minute, different places.

## Staying aware

I started thinking about this more when I noticed how my dad talks about his friends. He’ll mention someone from elementary school in passing, or casually update me on where a college friend lives, what they’re doing, who they’ve become. I’ve always admired how these relationships span decades. They were not preserved by constant communication or dramatic effort. He simply stayed aware. He checked in, remembered people as they moved through time. It wasn’t a coincidence. It was maintained, quietly and consistently.

Over the summer, I set a small goal for myself: reach out to one friend a day. Sometimes it was someone I hadn’t spoken to in months or years. Sometimes it was someone close. Sometimes I forgot. Other times, I hesitated. There wasn’t a script. I just wanted to know how they were doing, and I wanted them to be well. The responses varied. Some conversations picked up easily, and in 30 minutes, I was caught up on the past year of their life. Some seemed to end at “good, and you?” Some people were curious why I reached out, as if I shouldn’t have messaged them if there was no reason. Others just didn’t respond. What surprised me was how much had changed — new cities, interests, versions of people I thought I still knew. All from a single message. It made me realize how easily time passes without notice, and how little it takes for a connection to either reopen or disappear completely.

## Intention

This is where intention seems to lie, even though I’m still figuring out what that means. Not intention as effort, obligation, or emotional weight. More like a decision to remain present in someone else’s life, even lightly. An attempt to not let what you’ve built dissolve without being noticed. There’s no guarantee that the gesture will be reciprocated, because extending a hand doesn’t ensure it will be taken. But choosing not to reach out at all guarantees the distance stays. Intention, I’ve come to realize, isn’t intensity or constant communication. It’s the refusal to let time be the only thing acting on a relationship: a willingness to check in, to remember, and to stay reachable, even when nothing demands it.

---

At some point I realized 11:11 was never really about wishing. It was about time. When our spaces diverged, and our lives moved in different directions, we found a way to align our clocks. Twice a day, our timelines briefly overlapped, not through proximity, but through synchronization. Sometimes that minute held nothing more than a word. Sometimes it opened into conversation. Sometimes it didn’t. And that was okay. There were no expectations attached.

At 11:11, my phone buzzes. I type out “wish!” and press send. And yet, in the minutes leading up to it, I still find myself glancing at the clock every few moments, waiting for that brief passage to open again—when two timelines realign, if only for a minute.
